Ring escape and territory battle Shorts with a free, open-source simulator: Pixelbolt and 9 generators compared

Checked on 9 November 2026 by the makers of Pixelbolt, from each vendor's own pages: the browser tools that make ring-escape and bouncing-ball Shorts (BallTok, Satisfying Labs, BounceMatch, ReelBall, BallSimulator, BallEngine and Korpi) charge US$9.99–29 a month at regular price for their cheapest month-to-month paid plan, and WaveSpeed's browser maker is free. None of those eight, nor the free Python library physim, lists a grid territory battle, and Pixelbolt (free, MIT) wrote every frame of both clips below with one command each, the same bytes on every run, in 35 s and 102 s on one desktop PC. BallTok, Satisfying Labs and ReelBall also have watermarked free tiers; WaveSpeed needs no sign-up and adds no watermark. Pixelbolt writes no sound, has no live preview for matches this long and has no built-in search for a well-paced run: every other tool in the table makes sound, and most of the browser tools have a live preview. For battles between regions of a real map, see State-Royale under Also found. We ran Pixelbolt and physim on a Ryzen 9 7950X3D under Windows 11; the other eight we read without running them. Method, data and sources are below.

The two clips

Every pixel, word and number in both clips is drawn by Pixelbolt from the recipe. The counters and percentages fill in as the match runs; the question and the answer are lines of text in the recipe. The clips, the recipes and the data files linked on this page are MIT-licensed, like Pixelbolt.

How these matches were picked. Our agents tried variations with a small script and kept one of each. The ring: 24 start angles, of which 23 broke out within 40 s, after 12 to 73 bricks; this one (53) sits in the middle. The battle: 168 matches; at this length red came back to win 1 of 96 and drew 2, and this is the closest of the 93 it lost. The answer lines were written after those runs. Pixelbolt has no built-in search for a well-paced run; BallTok, ReelBall, BallSimulator and Satisfying Labs offer one.

Make the frames yourself: one command each

You need Node.js 20 or newer. In an empty folder holding the recipe, this writes every frame as a numbered PNG, plus a sequence.json with the match's numbers and a README.txt that says how to import the frames into a video editor:

npx -y pixelbolt@0.14.0 sequence ring-hits.bodies.json --out frames --fps 30 --size 1080x1920
npx -y pixelbolt@0.14.0 sequence comeback.bodies.json --out frames --fps 30 --size 1080x1920 --seconds 34 --background "#11131c"

Import the frames into your editor as an image sequence at 30 fps, at 100% scale, and add your music there. Or make an MP4 with ffmpeg (a separate program, one more line, run inside frames/); the line we used is in COMMANDS.txt. To check you got exactly our frames, save ring-frames-sha256.txt or territory-frames-sha256.txt next to the recipe and run cd frames && sha256sum -c ../ring-frames-sha256.txt. We got the same frames with Node.js 20, 22 and 24 on Windows; we haven't checked macOS or Linux, where the same pixels could be compressed into different PNG bytes, so if a hash differs there, compare the decoded pixels. The MP4's bytes depend on the ffmpeg build, so check the frames, not the MP4.

Change the match

  • Who starts with what: the territory's rows are a text picture of the board, one letter a cell (R red, B blue, . nobody's). 2 to 8 teams.
  • The balls: each body's at, velocity, speed (held, so balls never tire), team and fill colour.
  • The words: the hud entries. A team's name in braces fills in its cells, and with % its share, on every frame: the battle shows {red%} and {blue%}, and the ring's counter is {hits}, the cells of the ball's team. from and until show a line for some frames only, such as the answer at the end.
  • Twists: power-ups that split, grow, speed up, warp or magnetise a ball, breakable bodies and spinning blades are in Pixelbolt's rigid bodies guide, with seven presets, among them ring escape, spinning hoop and territory.
  • The answer: render, read how it ended in frames/sequence.json (figures.territory.counts has every frame's cells per team; figures.escaped the frame a ball left), then write the answer line and render again.
  • With an AI agent: Claude Code, Codex or Cursor can write and revise the recipe through Pixelbolt's MCP server (pixel_help topic bodies gives the format) or its command line. For matches like these (the battle took 102 s here) the agent runs the pixelbolt sequence command above, since one MCP call stops after 50 s by default (--time-limit changes it). Setup is in the README.

Also found, not in the table

  • State-Royale (Apache-2.0, Java, last changed January 2022): a command line that makes map "battle royale" videos, where regions of a real map conquer each other until one is left. It ranked first for "territory battle simulation video maker". Its bundled maps come from GADM, whose licence allows non-commercial use only without permission.
  • Ultimate Bouncing Ball APP (ranked in four of our five searches): €13.99 or more on itch.io for Windows, macOS and Linux (the download is a 9.4 kB zip), with your own song synced to the bounces, a circular escape hole and about 20 options.   • Algodoo: a free but proprietary 2D physics sandbox for Windows and macOS, with fluids. The pages we read don't mention video export.
  • Loop-Physics: one channel's own pipeline that renders physics Shorts, ring escapes among them, and posts them from GitHub Actions. It has no licence file, and its terms say it is "not offered, sold or licensed to anyone else".

When to use something else

  • You want music: each bounce playing the next note of a song. BallEngine (songs or MIDI), ReelBall and BallSimulator (your own MIDI files) do that; BallTok has a MIDI melody library and BounceMatch a MIDI melody mode; Satisfying Labs has a "Progressive Song" bounce-sound mode, and Pro users can add their own music; WaveSpeed and physim play a note per bounce. Pixelbolt makes no sound.
  • You want the tool to find a well-paced run for you. BallTok's auto-search tests 1,000 runs; ReelBall and BallSimulator have auto-search, and Satisfying Labs a "Simulation Finder" on Pro. With Pixelbolt you, or an agent, try variations yourself.
  • You want to tweak the run while you watch it, with no code. WaveSpeed (free, no sign-up), BallTok, Satisfying Labs, BounceMatch, ReelBall and BallEngine preview in the browser; Korpi has a preview step before it renders.
  • You want smooth, glowing shapes rather than pixel art. physim (free, MIT) if you're happy in Python; WaveSpeed if you want it free in the browser; the paid tools otherwise.
  • You want the videos posted for you. BallEngine posts to YouTube, TikTok, Facebook and Instagram from its Creator plan, with "Autopilot" on Pro; Korpi lists "Autopilot posting" too.
  • You want named balls, logos or flags in a fight. BounceMatch (logo and flag uploads), Satisfying Labs (named balls), ReelBall and BallEngine have battle modes built for that.
  • You want map battles between real countries or states. State-Royale, minding its map licence.

Where Pixelbolt fits

  • You want grid territory battles, where cells change hands, which no other tool in the table lists, or a whole video drawn as pixel art.
  • You want it free, with no account and no watermark, on your own computer, with the source under MIT (as physim is).
  • You want the same video every time: a recipe is a 3–6 KB text file that rebuilds every frame exactly, so you can keep it in git, share it, or change one number and see what changes (physim's seeds and WaveSpeed's seed links replay runs too).
  • You want an AI agent to make them, through Pixelbolt's MCP server or command line.

Limits: no sound; no live preview for long matches; no search for a well-paced run; pixel art only; the answer line is written into the recipe by hand after a run; the MP4's bytes depend on your ffmpeg build (the frames don't); and the runs on this page were on Windows only (Pixelbolt's tests run on Windows, macOS and Linux).
