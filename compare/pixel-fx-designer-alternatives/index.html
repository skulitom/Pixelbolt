Pixel FX Designer alternatives for pixel-art fire, smoke, explosions and water: 10 tools compared

Checked on 19 October 2026 by the makers of Pixelbolt and updated on 27 October and 15, 17 and 24 November: alternatives to Pixel FX Designer (CodeManu's particle-effect app, US$9.99 on Steam) include its maker's SpriteMancer and Fluid FX, the node-based Pixel Composer, the free browser apps PHOSPHOR and Pixel VFX Forge, BlastFX, the free Claude Code skill Codixel, Patch's free effects library pixelfx, and Pixelbolt; we read every tool's pages and docs and ran three, Pixelbolt 0.17.0, Codixel and pixelfx. Five of the nine alternatives (SpriteMancer, Fluid FX, Pixel Composer, PHOSPHOR and Pixelbolt) run a fluid or material simulation, which Pixel FX Designer does not; Pixel VFX Forge stacks stylised layers such as slashes, beams, shockwaves and lightning instead, Codixel draws its flame and smoke from moving noise and its fire wall from a Doom-style heat grid, and pixelfx's 244 ready-made effects are code that draws each frame or steps particles. A liquid simulation keeps track of the water: in Pixelbolt 0.17.0's dam-break preset, measured on 15 November 2026, 400 pixels of water let out from behind a plank stayed between 392 and 407 pixels in all 70 frames, never covered a pixel of the rock, basin or plank, and ended in all 64 columns, past the rock and resting on the basin floor. Pixelbolt is free and MIT-licensed like PHOSPHOR, Codixel and pixelfx, and of the ten, only Pixelbolt documents an MCP server that makes the effects (Pixel VFX Forge's agent skill renders with a Node script and uses Scenario's MCP server for character art). Measured on one Windows PC on 15 November 2026, its 0.17.0 release from npm compiled each of its 37 fluid presets (fire, smoke, explosions, magic, water, thick liquids, sand and lightning) three times to byte-identical files in 0.20–1.55 s per command and to the same recipes in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, and Pillow, a decoder independent of Pixelbolt's code, found only palette colours in every exported frame. Pixel Composer, PHOSPHOR, Pixel VFX Forge and pixelfx also document a way to render effects from a script, and Codixel's effects are functions that an agent's script calls; the others are used through their own window. We read seven of the other nine tools' pages and docs without running them (for Pixel VFX Forge, also its agent skill's source); Codixel and pixelfx we read and ran: four of Codixel's effects and six of pixelfx's each gave byte-identical files twice. We also had Claude make up to sixteen effects with Pixelbolt in four blocks and with pixelfx in two, from 10 to 23 November 2026: a blind judge preferred the results made with pixelfx in the first three blocks, and the fourth, on Pixelbolt 0.19.0, is reported below in the words we fixed before it ran. The short answer

  • You want to design particle effects by hand, with sliders, on Windows: Pixel FX Designer. Its Steam page says SpriteMancer, from the same maker, "includes most of the functionality of this software" and asks buyers to be aware of it before purchasing; it adds that Pixel FX Designer is still updated and maintained. Its latest stable version is 1.1.8.0, from April 2024.
  • You want particles, a fluid simulation, a node graph and 3D models in one Windows app: SpriteMancer (CodeManu, US$24.99 on Steam, still in Early Access; version 0.4.0.0 came out on 1 October 2026).
  • You want smoke, explosions, blood and magic from presets and sliders, on Windows: Fluid FX (CodeManu, US$9.99 on Steam). It runs a fluid simulation and exports a PNG sprite sheet.
  • You want a node graph with smoke and FLIP liquid simulations on Windows, macOS or Linux, and to re-run a saved project from a script: Pixel Composer (US$14.99; its source is MIT-licensed, but current builds are paid).
  • You want something free in the browser, with falling sand-style materials, rigid and soft bodies, a palette lock and JSON scenes an agent can write: PHOSPHOR (MIT, version 0.1).
  • You want GPU particles with post-effects such as palette mapping, pixelation, glow and outline, on Windows, macOS or Linux: BlastFX (US$19.99 on Steam or itch.io).
  • You want ready-made stylised combat and magic effects (sword slashes, beams, shockwaves, lightning, magic circles, damage numbers) to remix in the browser, preview on a character and download as MP4, GIF, a sprite sheet or a Godot 4 resource, with an agent skill that renders the same JSON recipes: Pixel VFX Forge (Scenario, free; announced on 26 October 2026). Its making-of describes it as a library of 42 hand-built effects.
  • You want Claude Code to turn your own reference picture into pixel art, give it bones and animate it, and to draw effects (flame, smoke, fire wall, fire breath, beams) and whole scenes with a one-file (92 KB) JavaScript library, checking each step with you: Codixel (free, MIT; a Claude Code skill created on 31 October 2026, with no releases yet). Its flame, smoke and fire are drawn from noise and a heat grid, not a fluid simulation, and none of its effects takes obstacles.
  • You want a large library of ready-made effects (244 in 21 categories, from fire, explosions, water, lightning and magic to UI, pickups, transitions and critters) that you recolour, reseed and play live in a browser game or export as sprite sheets with Aseprite-style JSON, plus a Claude Code skill that makes new ones: pixelfx (by Patch; free, MIT; created on 30 October 2026, version 0.3.1, no releases). Its smoke and glows are soft: in the transparent frames of the six effects we ran (what its sprite sheets are made from), 88–98% of the visible pixels were partly see-through, much of it its additive glow. It runs no fluid simulation and has no palette lock. Since 9 November its README and skill also offer to publish a browser game made with it on Patch, its maker's site where people play and remix browser games.
  • You want an AI agent (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Claude Desktop) to make and revise the effect over MCP (or a command line), seeing each result as an image, with the same files on every run and no colour outside your palette: Pixelbolt (free, MIT). You or the agent describe the effect in JSON; apart from its Powder lab, where you paint falling-sand materials live, there is no hand-drawing editor for effects.

What we measured on Pixelbolt

On 15 November 2026 we ran Pixelbolt 0.17.0, the newest release on npm (published 14 November), on one Windows 11 PC (AMD Ryzen 9 7950X3D, Node.js 24.19.0). We unpacked the npm package itself (npm pack pixelbolt@0.17.0, SHA-1 fb8f0349… as npm lists it), which has no dependencies, and ran every check on it with nothing installed. Pixelbolt's own CI ran its test matrix on the tagged commit (e1eb580) before the release was published, and passed (release run, test run); we did not re-run the tests. Each claim below links to its data.

  • Repeatable: each of the 37 fluid presets compiled three times, each in its own process, to byte-identical recipes and metadata. Rendering each recipe twice gave byte-identical sprite sheets, frames, atlases and other exports, and the 1× GIFs were byte-identical too (data). The 45 other effect examples (14 rigid-body scenes, 5 falling-sand sandboxes, 4 crystals, 2 wave loops and 20 particle and spell effects) each compiled twice to the same bytes (data).
  • The same in browsers: headless Chromium 156, Firefox 157 and WebKit 26.6 compiled all 37 fluid presets to the same recipe as Node. We compared Pixelbolt's revision ids, the first 12 hex digits of each recipe's SHA-256 (data).
  • Only palette colours: we decoded all 1,911 exported frames with Pillow 12.0.0, a decoder independent of Pixelbolt's code. Every visible pixel in every frame of every preset was one of the source's palette colours, and none was semi-transparent. The sprite sheet matched the frames pixel for pixel, and so did the 1× GIF, with the same frame timings (data).
  • Time: one pixelbolt compile command took 0.20–1.55 s per preset, Node's start-up included; the torch was the fastest and the water wheel, 224×176 pixels with rigid bodies, the slowest (data). Over MCP, where the server also renders the contact sheet it sends back, pixel_compile took 0.05–1.53 s (data). Timed on 15 November in back-to-back runs that alternated the two versions (20 runs each, medians, Node's start-up included), the ten presets 0.9.0 also had took 1.35 to 2.0 times as long to compile in 0.17.0: the campfire 336 ms against 247 ms, the waterfall 915 ms against 512 ms. Five of the ten sources are unchanged since 0.9.0 apart from formatting, so the difference is the compiler's (data, script, merge script).
  • An agent's round trip over MCP (data): we ran the server from the npm package. Its tools/list reply lists 8 tools in 20,905 bytes, and the fluid help topic is 3,306 bytes. pixel_compile returned a revision id and a PNG contact sheet for every preset. Sending the same source again returned the same revision id, and each id equalled the one computed from the recipe the command line compiled. pixel_render then exported the campfire's sprite sheet, frames and GIF in 0.08 s. Adding wind to the campfire source and compiling again took 0.16 s and gave a new revision.
  • Loops: 13 of the 37 presets loop. In all 13, the jump from the last frame back to the first changed no more pixels than the largest change between two ordinary neighbouring frames (data).

What we measured on Codixel

On 15 November 2026 we cloned Codixel at commit 604a9dc (its newest; it has no releases) and ran four of its built-in effects on the same Windows PC with Node.js 24.19.0. It met our rules for running another tool: it is MIT-licensed, and its one code file requires only Node's zlib and fs (to write and read PNG files), with no network calls, GPU use, dependencies or install scripts. Codixel ships no command line and no example scripts; its SKILL.md has Claude call the library from a script of its own. Each tool, and when to choose it over Pixelbolt

From each tool's own pages, read on 19 October 2026 (Pixel VFX Forge on 27 October); not run by us, except Codixel, read and run on 15 November 2026, and pixelfx, read and run on 17 November 2026.

Pixel FX Designer (CodeManu)

A desktop app for particle effects: emitters, motion, a timeline, physics such as attractors and gravity points, post-effects (glow, outline and others) and custom particle shapes, set with sliders, or with the particles' motion drawn with the mouse. You can import a palette of up to 256 colours and your own dithering, and make an animation loop. It exports a PNG sprite sheet or single frames. GIF export is marked experimental: its docs say to use it only with at most 60–120 frames, Scale 4 and a 400×400 canvas, as other settings may crash. A JSON export holds the emitter data so you can rebuild the effect in your engine. It comes with 1,000+ particle images and ready-made projects. It was released on Steam in 2018. The latest stable version is 1.1.8.0, from April 2024; a 2.0 beta from 2022 rewrites it with layers, a node graph and MP4 export. Its docs describe no command line or scripting, and none of its pages describes a fluid simulation.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want to shape a particle effect by eye, with sliders or by drawing the particles' motion with the mouse, on Windows and for little money, with your own palette of up to 256 colours and your own dither pattern, plus 1,000+ bundled particle images and ready-made projects.

SpriteMancer (CodeManu)

A newer, broader tool from the same maker, in Steam Early Access since January 2023; version 0.4.0.0 came out on 1 October 2026. It combines particles, emitters, a "Fluids" element (its docs say it "simulates fluid pressure" for smoke, fire and explosions), images, imported .obj models and a node graph. It exports PNG sprite sheets, a PNG per frame, GIF (without alpha), MP4 and WEBM; a render option fixes the random seed, and a Scale setting gives the render a pixelated look. Windows only. Its docs mention neither a palette lock nor a command line.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want one visual app for particles, fluids, 3D models and node effects, edited by hand, with video export.

Fluid FX (CodeManu)

A Windows app built on a "fluid/pressure simulation", powered by Mytino's Fluid Dynamics asset for GameMaker, whose own page says its physics and visualisation run on shaders. You pick one of 20+ presets, tune sliders, and export a trimmed PNG sprite sheet with alpha; you can render your own reference image into it, and export and import presets. GIF export is for previews. Version 1.1.0 is from January 2022. Its pages don't mention palette control, seeds or a command line. The effects they list are explosions, blood, smoke, dust and magic; water is not among them. Its Steam page carries the same SpriteMancer notice as Pixel FX Designer's.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want a quick smoke, blood or explosion sheet from one of 20+ presets, tuned with sliders on Windows, with alpha in a trimmed sheet and your own reference image rendered in.

Pixel Composer (MakhamDev)

A node-based compositor for pixel art and effects on Windows, macOS and Linux. Its simulation nodes include smoke (a domain with pressure, dissipation and emitters, written as GPU shaders in its source), FLIP liquids (with viscosity, friction and a FLIP-to-PIC ratio), particles and VFX with many affectors, rigid bodies, strands and verlet. Palette and dither nodes and indexed PNG export keep a pixel-art look. It renders sprite sheets and exports images, sequences, GIF, APNG, MP4 and WEBM. It can also import 3D objects with lighting and cameras, and .aseprite files. Its headless mode (-h) runs a saved project and triggers its export nodes, with variables set on the command line, and a persist or server (TCP port) mode keeps it open for console commands such as set and render. Its docs, labelled "pre 1.18", put persist and server in a separately downloaded CLI wrapper, since the app prints nothing to the console; the current source also parses them in the app. No MCP server is mentioned. Its source is MIT-licensed on GitHub, but current builds cost US$14.99, and building it yourself needs the GameMaker IDE. It needs an OpenGL GPU or integrated GPU.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want a visual node graph with its simulation nodes (including strands and verlet meshes), 3D objects with lighting and cameras, .aseprite import, video export and a GUI for building new effects. Pixelbolt's fluids stop at 128×128 and are written as JSON; Pixel Composer's docs state no domain limit.

PHOSPHOR VFX (Sinopsky)

A free, MIT-licensed effects studio that runs in a desktop browser with Canvas 2D, from itch.io or a downloaded folder. It simulates cellular materials (water, sand, smoke, stone, lava, fire, oil, steam and more, with chained reactions), rigid bodies that fracture, soft bodies, cloth, ropes and force fields, plus particle emitters with seven behaviours, on a keyframe timeline with 61 presets. Canvases run from 16 to 256 pixels. Its post-effects include a palette lock with ordered dithering (PICO-8, Game Boy, NES or your own), posterize, outline, chromatic aberration, scanlines and bloom. It exports PNG frames, sprite sheets, looping GIFs, scene JSON, an HTML player and layers as a ZIP. Scenes are plain JSON. Command-line tools render a scene to a sprite sheet, frames or GIF through the Chrome or Edge you have installed, validate it and summarise it, and a SKILL.md explains the format to AI agents. Its page says the engine is deterministic and seed-based, and mentions no MCP server. The download is version 0.1, and its one devlog is from August 2026.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want a free visual editor in the browser for physics effects on a keyframe timeline, including soft bodies, which none of Pixelbolt 0.17.0's schemas describe; post-effects Pixelbolt lacks (bloom, chromatic aberration, scanlines); and still JSON scenes an agent can write. Pixelbolt 0.17.0 also has falling sand, rigid bodies that break, cloth and ropes, but written as JSON rather than edited by hand. Of the tools here, PHOSPHOR is the closest to Pixelbolt's approach.

BlastFX (MNKY)

A desktop app for Windows, macOS and Linux, on Steam since 2018, with GPU-accelerated particle emitters and render layers, each with a chain of post-effects: Palette Map, Gradient Map, Glow, Outline, Pixelate, Blur, Seamless Tile and others. It exports sprite sheets, PNG sequences and GIF, and video: uncompressed AVI per its store page, or MP4, WebM and AVI through a separately installed FFmpeg per its docs. Its docs give each emitter a random seed and a seamless-looping option. It costs US$19.99 on Steam or itch.io, needs OpenGL 3.3, and its demo can't export. Its pages describe no fluid simulation and no command line.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want GPU particles with soft glows and blur that you then pixelate and palette-map, edited by hand, with video export.

Pixel VFX Forge (Scenario)

Added on 27 October 2026, the day after Scenario announced it: a free page in the browser and a downloadable agent skill for stylised combat and magic effects. Its making-of describes it as a library of 42 hand-built effects in five groups (combat, magic, elements, feedback and ambient) that you remix with words or an inspector. The words are read by rules, not a language model: 34 forms (explosion, slash, beam, portal, heal, level up and more), 18 elements (fire, ice, poison, holy, shadow and more) and modifiers for size, speed, colour and style. An effect is a JSON recipe of layers, 22 types in all, each writing a heat field over its own time window. The renderer cuts the heat into hard bands of a hue-shifted 4–7 colour ramp, optionally with a 4×4 Bayer dither, then removes stray pixels, fills one-pixel holes, draws inner or outer outlines and can lock every colour to a retro palette. Loops close because periodic settings complete whole cycles, and a one-shot's impact frame is found automatically or set in the recipe. The page previews each effect on a caster or a target in a 384×216 scene, with a hit flash, knockback and screen shake timed to the impact; that stage art was generated through Scenario's MCP server. It encodes MP4 in the browser and exports GIF, sprite sheets and a game-ready ZIP that includes a Godot 4 SpriteFrames resource. The agent skill carries the same engine and a Node script: an agent writes a recipe, renders it locally, checks the frames and iterates, and can preview the effect over its own character image. Canvases run from 8 to 512 pixels a side and from 1 to 120 frames. Its recipe reference says unknown fields are ignored, so a misspelt setting is skipped rather than reported. The announcement calls the skill open-sourced, but the download carries no licence file or notice, and Scenario's MIT-licensed skills repository does not include it. We read its page, its making-of and the skill's source; we did not run it.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want stylised combat and magic effects picked from a library of ready-made recipes and previewed on a character in the browser, with MP4 for posting or a Godot 4 resource to import. Its 22 layer types include sword slashes, beams, shockwave rings, lightning, magic circles, impact stars, claw marks, shields and damage numbers. Pixelbolt added eight such shapes in 0.9.2 (28 October 2026), along with a Godot 4 export, and 0.17.0 has ten kinds of shape layer: rings, slashes, beams, lightning bolts, magic circles, impact stars, fireball bursts, water streams, auras round a sprite and trails behind a sword tip. Its skill lets an agent render the same recipes from a Node script, without an MCP server.

Codixel (mankidev)

Added on 15 November 2026: a free, MIT-licensed Claude Code skill on GitHub, created on 31 October 2026 (187 stars on 15 November), with no releases or tags; we read and ran its newest commit, 604a9dc from 2 November. It has two parts. Its 51 KB SKILL.md tells Claude to work from a reference picture the user supplies rather than draw complex subjects from imagination: remove the background, turn it into pixels, give it bones (P.Rig, or P.bend along a spine), animate it, and show each step and ask before going on; game files wait until the user approves a preview. It also sets style rules (hue-shifted ramps, flat bands with a dither strip only where two bands meet, outlines in the sprite's own colours) and asks Claude to take motion from real physics, with formulas. Its 92 KB core/pixel.js is a dependency-free JavaScript library for Node or a browser with those image tools, scene builders (sky, clouds, rock, trees, grass, water reflections, mist, and gems drawn by following light through the stone) and effects its docs say are "taken from finished games": a flame, a smoke column, a fire wall, fire breath, a smoke explosion and beams. The flame and smoke are a shape plus moving noise, cut into colour bands; the fire wall and fire breath spread heat on a grid the way Doom's fire does. Nothing in the file simulates a fluid, and no effect takes obstacles (fire breath takes a floor line below which it draws nothing). It writes RGBA PNG frames, a plain-grid PNG sprite sheet and GIF; there is no command line, MCP server, atlas file or engine export. Its README shows fire, smoke, beam, explosion and water results as GIFs; the scripts that made them are not in the repository. Installing it as the README says (git clone into Claude Code's skills folder) downloaded 69.5 MiB, nearly all of it images. We ran four of its effects; the numbers are above.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want Claude Code to turn your own picture into a pixel-art character or creature, rig it and animate it, with a method that stops for your approval at every step, or to build whole scenes in code (trees, rock, water reflections, skies, gems). It is one readable JavaScript file that also runs in a browser, and its smoke fades through five alpha levels: every visible smoke pixel in our run was partly see-through, while all 37 of Pixelbolt 0.17.0's fluid presets used only opaque pixels in our run.

pixelfx (Patch)

Added on 17 November 2026: a free, MIT-licensed library of pixel-art effects with a Claude Code plugin (not related to CodeManu's Pixel FX Designer), on GitHub as patch-gg/pixelfx, created on 30 October 2026 (7,929 stars and 1,038 forks on 17 November). It has no releases or tags; its package.json says 0.3.1. We read and ran its newest commit, 5294518 from 12 November. It holds 244 effects in 21 categories (moves, fire, explosions, water, ice, lightning, sci-fi, melee, impacts, projectiles, spawn and death, arcane, holy, shadow, status, nature, weather, UI, pickups, transitions and critters), each one JavaScript file with no images. An effect either draws the frame at a time t from t alone, so its loops close, or steps particles, debris and lights at 120 Hz from a seed. It draws opaque, dithered or truly translucent pixels into one buffer and additive light into another, and every effect takes a palette ramp, a seed, a flip, an angle and its own options. The 61 effects with a character, and 12 "moves" that chain effects into whole attacks, also say what a stock 24×38 character does around them: a wind-up, a freeze on contact, a target that rocks back. You play effects live in a browser game, or render them with its command-line scripts to a contact sheet, a transparent PNG sprite sheet with Aseprite-style JSON (frame rectangles and durations, plus fps, loop, columns, pivot and the act's timing), which its README says Phaser's atlas loader and the Aseprite importers for Godot and Unity read, or GIF and MP4 previews made through ffmpeg. Its README credits the method to Hugo Duprez's article on making pixel-art effects with Claude, and its particle code and some effects (the campfire among them) adapt his open-source effects at Sprite Fusion. Nothing in it simulates a fluid, and no effect takes obstacle shapes (some bounce particles off a floor line). Its skill, installed as a Claude Code plugin from the repository's own marketplace or by copying a folder, tells Claude to look for an existing effect first and to make new ones by drafting, rendering, looking at the contact sheet and refining; a check script flags unseeded randomness, loop seams and slow frames. pixelfx is made by Patch, a site where people play and remix browser games. Since 9 November its README and skill say how to publish a browser game made with the effects on Patch, with Patch's command line (pch) and Patch Desktop open on a Windows PC; the skill tells Claude to offer this once and to go ahead only on the person's yes. Since 12 November its README also links a pixelfx page on Patch for starting a browser game from an effect. Nothing in the library, its effects or its render scripts contacts Patch or any other network service. We ran six of its effects; the numbers are above.

Choose it over Pixelbolt when you want a large library of ready-made effects for a whole game rather than a few to build on: 244 of them in 21 categories, including melee swings, impacts, projectiles, status effects, UI pop-ups, pickups, screen transitions and critters (Pixelbolt 0.17.0 ships 37 fluid presets and 45 other effect examples). You can drop them into a browser game as code and recolour, mirror, turn or reseed them as it runs, or export sprite sheets with JSON, and 61 of them, with the 12 moves, carry the timing of a character's wind-up, the freeze on contact and the target's knock-back. Its smoke and glows are soft, with additive light that Pixelbolt does not have: 88–98% of the visible pixels in the six effects we ran were partly see-through, while all 37 of Pixelbolt 0.17.0's fluid presets used only opaque pixels in our run (its see-through poison gas, an effect example, uses three fixed translucent colours). In our agent bench, a blind judge preferred the effects Claude made with pixelfx over those made with Pixelbolt 0.15.0 and 0.17.0, and still preferred pixelfx's wall torch, lightning strike and electric arc to Pixelbolt 0.19.0's.

When to choose Pixelbolt

When an AI agent should make, check and change the effect over MCP, seeing each result as an image: the agent writes a few kilobytes of JSON (palette, obstacles, emitters and how fields map to colours), compiles it, looks at the contact sheet it gets back, and changes one setting at a time, with the same file every time. (PHOSPHOR and Pixel VFX Forge also take agent-written JSON, through command-line scripts rather than MCP; Codixel's agent writes JavaScript that calls its library, and pixelfx's skill has the agent write or edit an effect's JavaScript file and render it with a command-line script.) The result is an ordinary Pixelbolt recipe, so the same agent can inspect it, patch single pixels, and export sprite sheets, atlases, GIFs or frames for a video editor. It is free and needs only Node.js: no account, API key or network service. Install it from the GitHub repository, or as pixelbolt.mcpb for Claude Desktop from the latest release.

Pixelbolt's limits

  • Small and short where no preset fits. In our agent bench, on the six tasks with no finished preset, agents using Pixelbolt 0.19.0 built effects with a median longest side of 48 px and 11.5 frames; on the same six tasks, the results made with pixelfx had medians of 136 px and 24 frames.
  • No hand-editing tool for new effects. A new effect is a JSON source that you or an agent write. The studio's Fluid lab and Effects lab (in its docs; not used for this page) run a preset in your browser with a few sliders and download the result, and its Powder lab lets you paint falling-sand materials live, but there is no emitter editor, timeline or node graph like the desktop apps above.
  • Ten kinds of shape layer. Spells and hits are built from ten kinds of shape layer (rings, slashes, beams, bolts, magic circles, stars, fireball bursts, water streams, auras and trails; all ten compiled in our run) plus particle emitters. Pixel VFX Forge has 22 layer types.
  • Young and bounded. Fluid effects first shipped in 0.9.0 on 18 October 2026, and 0.17.0 came out on 14 November. A fluid source is refused if it needs more than 32,768 liquid particles, 4,096 sparks or about 400 million cell updates, and flows are capped at 32 pixels a frame.
  • Small and 2D. A fluid grid is at most 128×128 cells, one per world pixel (a scene with rigid bodies may draw each cell as a block of 2–8 pixels), and an animated recipe at most 256 pixels a side and 256 frames. None of its schemas or docs describe soft bodies or 3D models.
  • Hard-edged pixels only. Every pixel an effect source compiles to is one of the palette's colours. All 37 fluid presets use only opaque ones (measured: no semi-transparent pixel); a palette may include translucent colours (the poison-spells effect example uses three), and a recipe layer can be given one opacity by hand, but there is no blur, smooth alpha falloff or additive blending; glows are drawn with dither.
  • Baked frames for engines. Effects compile to sprite frames, not engine particles. A web page can run a fluid or a falling-sand sandbox live with createLiveFluid or createLivePowder, as the live fluid toy does with fluids, but there is no live runtime for game engines.
  • Video is frames only. Pixelbolt writes numbered PNGs and the FFmpeg command for a video; it encodes no video itself.
  • Tool list in context. The MCP server's tools/list reply is 20,905 bytes on 0.17.0 (135,553 on 0.9.0; data, 0.9.0 data), which an agent pays for in context; the fluid help topic is another 3,306 bytes.
  • Measured on one machine. Our timings are from one Windows desktop CPU. Pixelbolt's CI runs its tests, fluid golden hashes included, on Linux, Windows and macOS.
